Brand system · Article Forms · v1.0

Range without randomness

How every piece we publish sounds unmistakably like us, and no two feel like the same article wearing a new hat.

01 · The core idea

Form follows diagnosis

The trap is thinking variety means shaking things up so readers don't get bored. That produces gimmicks, and Dorothy can smell a gimmick.

We vary the form of an article for the same reason a good doctor varies the treatment: because the problem in front of us is shaped differently this time.

A question with a surprising one-word answer wants a different shape than a decision with two honest paths, which wants a different shape than a piece of mail that needs translating line by line. Match the form to the reader's actual need and range happens on its own. It also makes every piece more useful, which is the whole point.

So the discipline is not "be unpredictable." It is "diagnose first, then choose the form that serves this reader best." Predictability is what happens when we stop diagnosing and start defaulting.

02 · The two lists

What never changes, and what must

Consistency lives in the voice and the standards. Range lives in the structure. Most content operations get this backwards: they lock the structure and let the voice drift, which is why their archive reads like one long article nobody finished.

Invariant · never changes

The signature

  • The voice. Candid, kind, clear, close-up, musical. One wink, aimed at the system.
  • Dorothy is the reader. Second person, present tense, no "beneficiaries."
  • The gate. True, useful, actionable, clear. Verified and dated.
  • Fast orientation. She knows within a few seconds what this is and whether it's for her. Never throat-clearing.
  • One next step at the end. Specific, doable in five minutes.
  • Blame the system, never her.
  • The typography, palette, and compliance footer.
Variable · must change

The arrangement

  • The form. Ten in the repertoire, chosen by diagnosis.
  • The opening move. Eight to pick from. Never the same one twice running.
  • Length. 200 words to 2,000. Length is a choice, not a habit.
  • Where the answer sits. Sometimes sentence one. Sometimes after a 60-word scene that earns it.
  • The furniture. Some pieces have subheads and lists. Some are unbroken prose. Some are a worksheet with almost no prose at all.
  • Register. Some pieces are warm and funny. Some are quiet and serious. The voice is the same person in both moods.

One honest tension worth naming: the style guide says "answer in the first two sentences." The truer version of that rule is orient her immediately. A scene that runs 60 words before landing the answer still orients her, because she can tell instantly that this is about her problem. What's banned is the warm-up paragraph that tells her nothing.

03 · The repertoire

Ten forms, each with a different shape

Think of these the way a musician thinks about a repertoire, not the way a factory thinks about a mold. Each has a natural arc, drawn beside it. Pick by diagnosis, never by habit.

1. The Straight Answer

When she has one urgent question with a real answer

Arc: answer, then why, then the one exception, then what to do today. The workhorse. Use it for maybe a third of everything, never more.

OpeningNo, you probably don't have to sign up for Part B at 65 if you're still working and your employer has 20 or more people. There's one trap, and it catches people with health savings accounts.

2. The Autopsy

When a costly mistake is common and preventable

Arc: start at the damage, then walk backwards to the decision that caused it, then hand her the fork in the road while she can still take the other path. Runs on curiosity instead of urgency, so it never needs to shout.

OpeningThe bill was $4,700. Ray had done everything right, or so he thought, and he had the paperwork to prove it. Here is where it actually went wrong, eleven months before that bill arrived.

3. The Side-by-Side

When there is no right answer, only an honest trade

Arc: name both paths in her language, give each its best case made sincerely, then say plainly who each one is wrong for. Ends with the question that decides it, not with our recommendation.

OpeningTwo paths, and the ads only ever show you one of them. Here is the honest version of both, including the part where each one is a bad fit.

4. The Walkthrough

When the task has real steps in a real order

Arc: chronological, one step per beat, with the phone number or date she needs sitting right inside the step where she needs it. She should be able to do this with the page open beside her.

OpeningTwenty minutes, one phone call, and a number you already have in a drawer. That's the whole job. Here's the order to do it in.

5. The Myth Demolition

When something everyone believes is costing people money

Arc: state the belief fairly and explain why it sounds right, show exactly where it breaks, then replace it with the true version. Never smug. The reader believed this, and she was reasonable to.

Opening"Medicare is free at 65." Almost everyone believes some version of this, and it's easy to see why: you paid in for forty years. Here's what those forty years actually bought, and what they didn't.

6. The Field Note

When a real scene teaches better than an explanation

Arc: McPhee mode. Open inside one specific moment, stay there longer than feels comfortable, then pull back to what it means for her. The rarest form and the most memorable. Use sparingly or it becomes shtick.

OpeningThe shoebox held 44 pieces of mail. Ruth had saved every one since March, because she couldn't tell which mattered, so she kept them all. We sorted them at her kitchen table in about nine minutes.

7. The Decoder

When a real document is the thing confusing her

Arc: show the actual page, then translate it line by line in her language. Almost nobody does this, and it is the single most useful thing we can publish. Her mail, her statement, her letter from Social Security.

OpeningThis is the letter Medicare sends about your Part B premium. Four of its paragraphs don't matter to you at all. One of them decides what you pay next year. Let's go line by line.

8. The Short Answer

When the honest answer is 200 words and padding it would be a lie

Arc: answer, one clarifier, next step. Done. Publishing a short piece when short is right is itself a signal of quality, because everyone else inflates. Length variety is a real part of range.

OpeningYes, you can change plans every fall between October 15 and December 7. That's the whole answer. Here's the one thing worth knowing before you do.

9. The Worksheet

When she needs a tool, not an essay

Arc: the piece is the artifact. Minimal prose, maximum blanks. Printable. She fills it in and walks into a doctor's office or a phone call holding it. This is where the workbook library begins.

OpeningPrint this. Fill in the four blanks. Take it to the phone call. Everything you'll be asked is on it, in the order they'll ask.

10. The Letter

When the subject is heavy and the reader needs a person, not a page

Arc: written as correspondence, signed by Austin or Andrew. For the topics that are actually about fear: a spouse's coverage, running out of money, what happens when one of you dies. No jokes. No lists. Just one person talking to another.

OpeningThis is the question people wait until the very end of a call to ask, usually quietly. I want to answer it here so you don't have to ask it out loud.

04 · The first ten seconds

Eight ways in

Predictability is most fatal in the opening, because that's where she decides whether we're worth her time. The form sets the arc; the opening move sets the tone. Rotate deliberately.

MoveWhat it sounds like
The answer"No, and here's the exception that catches people."
The scene"The shoebox held 44 pieces of mail."
The number"$4,700. That was the part nobody warned him about."
The confession"We got this wrong on a call last month, so we're writing it down properly."
The belief"Medicare is free at 65. Almost everyone believes some version of this."
Her own words"Someone asked it exactly this way on Tuesday: 'Do I have to do anything, or does it just start?'"
The object"This is the letter Social Security sends in the month you turn 64 and a half."
The date"December 7 is the deadline. Most people hear about it on December 6."

05 · The discipline

Five rules that make range automatic

Good intentions drift. These are mechanical, which means they still work on a busy Thursday.

  1. Diagnose before you draft

    Write the reader's problem in one sentence at the top of the file, then pick the form that fits it. If the form was chosen before the diagnosis, start over. This single habit prevents most sameness.

  2. Never the same form twice in a row

    And never the same opening move twice in a row, even across different forms. Two Straight Answers back to back is how an archive starts sounding like a template.

  3. Keep the log

    content/published-log.md records date, title, form, opening move, and word count for every piece. Thirty seconds per article, and it makes drift visible instead of invisible.

  4. Quarterly, read the archive out loud in order

    Sameness is inaudible one article at a time and obvious across ten. If three pieces in a row open at the same distance from the reader, the next one changes shape on purpose.

  5. Retire and add forms

    The repertoire is not sacred. If the Autopsy has carried four pieces this quarter, bench it. When a reader's problem doesn't fit any of the ten, invent the eleventh and write it down here. A living repertoire beats a fixed one.

06 · The audit

Three tests for a predictable draft

The shuffle test

Take the first sentence off this draft and the first sentence off the last three pieces. Shuffle them. Could a stranger tell which is which?

If no, the openings have collapsed into one voice-shaped rut.

The skeleton test

Write only the subheads of this piece and the previous one, side by side. Do they have the same rhythm and the same number of beats?

If yes, we templated without noticing.

The surprise test

Name the one thing in this piece Dorothy would repeat to her daughter tonight. A number, a line, a reframe.

If nothing comes to mind, it is competent and forgettable. Not good enough to publish.

All three failures have the same cure, and it isn't stylistic: go back to the diagnosis. Predictable writing is nearly always a symptom of a question we didn't think hard enough about. When the reader's problem is genuinely understood, its shape suggests the article's shape, and sameness stops being a risk.

07 · The summary

One line to remember

Same voice, same standards, same person talking. Different shape every time, because every reader's problem has a different shape.
